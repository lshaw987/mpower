You know those moments... shoulders tight, mind racing, energy flat.

You're running on empty, and even working out how to recharge feels like one more decision you don't have the bandwidth for. That's exactly why I believe in having a self-care menu - a pre-prepared, personal list of go-to actions you can turn to when you're out of sync. No thinking. No planning. Just choosing.

## How it works

Much like ordering from a menu, you choose what's going to nourish you (mind, body or soul) based on how much time and energy you have in the moment. It sounds light-hearted on the surface, but the thinking behind it is solid. The American Psychological Association describes decision fatigue - the mental drain from making too many choices - as something that erodes our ability to make good decisions and increases stress. Taking decisions off the table when you're depleted turns a simple list into a real psychological aid. Structure without rigidity. Choice without pressure.

## The three columns

**Mind.** Quick resets like a breathing exercise, a brain dump onto paper, or a two-minute break from your phone. Deep breathing calms the fight-or-flight response and brings cortisol down. Writing your thoughts down clears the mental overload. Micro-practices that, over time, create macro shifts in how you handle stress.

**Body.** Your body carries your stress too, often before your brain notices. Movement in small doses releases endorphins. A short walk, a stretch, a glass of water can interrupt the stress loop and reset your nervous system. Research out of Stanford suggests walking boosts creative thinking by up to 60%, so what feels like a break might be the moment your best idea turns up.

**Soul.** The part of you that craves meaning, joy and connection - the things that remind you who you are outside your responsibilities. Music that lifts you. Calling someone who gets you. Stepping into the sunshine for a few deep breaths. Positive connection and sensory pleasure light up the brain's reward system and shift us out of survival mode.

## Three prompts to start

Before you fill anything in, sit with these three questions. They're on the template too.

1. What helps you slow down and breathe a little easier?
2. What rituals or activities leave you feeling strong, clear, and energised?
3. What makes your heart sing and brings you joy?

## The menu grid

The template is a simple grid. Three columns across the top for Mind, Body and Soul. Three rows down the side for how much time you've actually got - 10 minutes, 30 minutes or 60 minutes.

| Time I have | Mind | Body | Soul |
|---|---|---|---|
| **10 minutes** | | | |
| **30 minutes** | | | |
| **60 minutes** | | | |

Nine boxes. Each one holds two or three real, doable things you already know work for you. When you're flat, find the row that matches the time you have and pick one thing from any column. There's a notes and resources column down the side for the things that don't fit a box - the playlist, the podcast, the person.

Here's how a couple of rows might look, using the things I reach for.

| Time I have | Mind | Body | Soul |
|---|---|---|---|
| **10 minutes** | A breathing exercise · two minutes off the phone | Stretch · drink a glass of water | Step into the sunshine for a few deep breaths |
| **30 minutes** | Brain dump onto a blank page | A short walk | Call someone who gets you · music that lifts you |

## The real magic

It doesn't need to be fancy. A self-care menu is about intention - knowing in advance what supports you best, so when the going gets tough you don't have to work it out from scratch. You just pick something. And start.

## Fill yours in

Take five minutes. Fill the grid with real, doable things that serve you - the stuff you'll actually do, not the aspirational list. Keep it somewhere visible. And when life gets heavy (which it will), don't try to fix everything. Choose one thing from your menu, and begin.
