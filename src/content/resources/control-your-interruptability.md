You don't have a time problem. You have an interruptability problem.

Most leaders I work with aren't short on time. They're constantly available. And that availability is quietly destroying their ability to lead - to think, to decide well, to do the work only they can do.

Here's how to take part in what I call the Great Switch Off, and take back control of your time, energy and focus.

## Every interruption has a cost

You don't just "pick back up" where you left off. Every interruption makes you context-switch, and that's expensive. The figure I use is 27 minutes - what it takes to reclaim your focus, restart the thinking and rebuild the depth after one ping. And it's never quite the same.

Count the pings in an average morning and do the maths. It gets uncomfortable fast.

## You get to choose

Interruptability is a leadership choice. The best leaders I know manage this well for themselves and for the people around them. How you deal with interruption is not a personality trait. Not "just how it is". Not "the price of being busy".

So what will you choose?

## Five ways to take back control

**1. Design your no-interruption zones.** Block time when you're only available to yourself, and protect it fiercely.

**2. Be intentional about responsiveness.** You don't need to reply instantly. Decide when you respond, rather than reacting to whoever pinged last.

**3. Manage your signals.** A green status, notifications on, no "in focus time" sign on the door or desk - all of that says "I'm available". Change the signals and people will read them.

**4. Build interruption pathways that aren't you.** Who can answer the question or make the decision before it gets to you? If everything comes through you, you're a bottleneck, however good your intentions.

**5. Protect your deep work time.** That's your job. Work out what works for you. For me it's no meetings on Mondays, or before 10am. Time booked in my calendar for myself is committed to just like I would to an external person.

## The Great Switch Off - a checklist

Start small, with your notifications, and work outward. Tick them off over a week. The PDF is this list with room to write what you changed.

**Your devices**

- [ ] Turn off every non-essential notification, on every device. If it doesn't need immediate action, it doesn't need to interrupt you.
- [ ] Remove badges and pop-ups. Those red dots and "pings" are designed to pull you in.
- [ ] Create check-in windows - set times when you choose to look at email, Teams and messages.
- [ ] Go Do Not Disturb when you want to do deep work.

**Your week and the people around you**

- [ ] Block your no-interruption zones in the calendar for the week ahead.
- [ ] Decide your response times - intentional, not reactive.
- [ ] Change your signals - status set to busy, a sign on the door or desk when you're in focus time.
- [ ] Name who can answer or decide before it gets to you, and let them.
- [ ] Set your own deep-work rule (mine is Mondays and before 10am) and commit to it like an external appointment.

## Available, on purpose

Controlling your interruptability is about being intentional. You can still be there for people - you just decide when, instead of letting the pings decide for you.
